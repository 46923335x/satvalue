Why Denominate Markets in Bitcoin?

Every price is a ratio. Changing the unit of account changes the story that price tells.

When we say Apple trades at $250 per share, we are not measuring an intrinsic property of Apple. We are expressing the value of one share relative to a particular unit of account: the U.S. dollar.

This is so familiar that it often goes unnoticed. Investors in the United Kingdom naturally think about wealth in pounds. Europeans think in euros. Japanese investors think in yen. The same company, on the same day, can have different price histories depending entirely on which currency is used to measure it.

The underlying asset has not changed. Only the numeraire has.

Economics has long recognized this distinction, but financial media rarely does. Returns are usually reported in domestic currency, reinforcing the assumption that the chosen unit of account is neutral. It is not.

Bitcoin introduces something different. Unlike national currencies, it is not associated with a country or monetary authority. It is a global monetary asset. Because it belongs everywhere, it also belongs nowhere; there is no natural financial center where markets are quoted in Bitcoin by default.

What happens when Bitcoin becomes the numeraire?

The Same Asset, a Different Story

Expressing a dollar-denominated stock price in Bitcoin is an ordinary currency conversion. The relationship can be written so the units cancel directly:

Stock PriceUSD × USDBTC = Stock PriceBTC

The USD denominator in the first fraction cancels with the USD numerator in the second, leaving the stock price expressed in BTC. The calculation changes the numeraire, not the underlying asset.

Nothing about the underlying business changes. Only the unit of measurement changes. Yet the conclusions can be dramatically different.

An investment that appears to have compounded at 10% annually in U.S. dollars may have lost more than 99% of its value when denominated in Bitcoin over the same period. That is not a contradiction. It means Bitcoin appreciated faster than the investment itself.

Instead of asking only whether an investment created wealth in dollar terms, we can ask whether it created wealth relative to an increasingly scarce monetary asset. Those are not the same question.

Why This Matters

Investment performance is always assessed against some standard. Cash is one. Treasury bills are another. Inflation-adjusted purchasing power is another. Professional investors routinely compare active managers with market indices.

Choosing Bitcoin as the numeraire follows the same comparative logic. If Bitcoin has become a significant store of value, every investment carries an implicit opportunity cost: not owning Bitcoin.

BTC-denominated analysis makes that opportunity cost explicit. It reveals which investments increased purchasing power in Bitcoin terms and which merely kept pace with—or lagged—the changing monetary standard.

A Different Perspective on Wealth

Across much of Bitcoin’s history, a remarkable pattern has emerged. When measured in Bitcoin, most major conventional asset classes have declined substantially. Equities, bonds, real estate, precious metals, and many successful technology companies have lost purchasing power in Bitcoin terms.

This does not mean those assets became fundamentally worse businesses or inherently poor investments. It means Bitcoin holders accumulated wealth at a rate that exceeded most conventional investments during those periods.

A rising dollar price represents increasing wealth relative to dollars. It does not necessarily represent increasing wealth under every monetary standard.

Why kS?

Bitcoin has appreciated enough that quoting everyday asset prices directly in BTC often produces awkward decimals. Instead of writing 0.002347 BTC, SatValue quotes prices in kS, where 1 kS = 1,000 satoshis.

It is simply a more practical unit of account. Financial markets comfortably quote values in thousands, millions, or billions of dollars depending on context. kS provides a readable scale while remaining directly tied to Bitcoin.

As Bitcoin’s purchasing power changes, quoting directly in satoshis may eventually become more natural. For now, kS provides a useful middle ground.

The Purpose of This Site

This is not an argument that every investor should own Bitcoin, nor is it an attempt to replace traditional financial analysis.

The purpose is simpler: changing the numeraire changes the questions we ask.

Looking at markets through Bitcoin reveals relationships that are invisible when everything is measured in dollars. It highlights opportunity cost, exposes changes in purchasing power, and offers a different way to think about capital allocation across time.

Economists have long understood that measurement shapes interpretation. SatValue is an invitation to explore markets through a different measure.
